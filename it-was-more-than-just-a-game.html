It Was More Than Just a Game

Reflections on technology, independent thinking and the purpose of education.

Didula Weerasekara · 26 November 2026 · 3 min read

It was raining heavily outside. The sound of the rain filled the classroom, while the raindrops striking the windows created a rather peculiar sound. Having already completed our term syllabus and assessments, I had the freedom to change my lesson plan for the day. So, I decided to play Hangman with my students.

While we were playing, one boy suddenly asked, “Teacher, can we use our mobile phones to find a word?”

I wondered why.

Is it really necessary to use a mobile phone to find a word? What if we go through our storybooks and portfolios to find one?

The student soon realised that he, too, was capable of doing it by himself.

As an educator, that struck me. Look at how technology may have diminished our students' abilities — or perhaps not their abilities, but their confidence in those abilities.

There is a significant difference between being incapable of doing something and believing that we are incapable of doing it.

This is precisely why we need to pay greater attention to skills development in the classroom. Merely feeding students information is not enough. We should allow them to acquire relevant knowledge alongside the skills they need to thrive in the world.

Technology itself is not necessarily the problem. The question is whether we are using it to extend our capabilities or allowing it to replace them.

Regardless of what our students choose to do in the future, they should be capable of doing something meaningful, not only for themselves but also for the society in which they live.

So, dear parents and teachers, before pushing children towards what we expect them to become, perhaps we should pay closer attention to what they are capable of becoming. Given the right opportunities, they might even go beyond our expectations.

Coming back to the game, I noticed how much the students craved experiences like this and how much they needed such exposure within the classroom.

They exchanged ideas, thought together, made decisions and competed with one another. They were learning to deal with situations in which they could not always predict the outcome.

And let us not forget that the world is not a level playing field. At some point, these children will have to face that reality. They will encounter competition, disagreements, failures and circumstances that demand independent judgement.

How well are we preparing them for such a world?

Playing a language game in the classroom, therefore, is not necessarily beneficial only to their language studies. It can also provide opportunities to develop the skills they need in life.

Perhaps we should occasionally move away from our textbooks, play a game and give our students the freedom to think, interact, make mistakes and discover what they are capable of.

That day, I changed my lesson plan simply because it was raining heavily outside. Yet, what unfolded in the classroom reminded me of something far more important.

Education is not merely about what we put into our students' minds. It is also about what we enable them to do with their own minds.

After all, what are we educating them for?
